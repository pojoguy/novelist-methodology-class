# Grounding fixtures — Danish Cemetery (POV-blind)

## Terms in this folder

**For non-technical readers:** plain-language definitions for words used below.  
**Full glossary:** [`docs/glossary.md`](../../docs/glossary.md).

| Term | Plain language |
|------|----------------|
| **Grounding** | Photos, maps, and notes you file about a **real place** so you (and your tools) can check prose against fact — not chat memory. |
| **Street View** | Google's ground-level photos of a road or site — what it actually looks like from the shoulder. |
| **POV-blind** | **You** know where the scene is; the **character on the page** does not — and that can be correct. |
| **Model prior** | What the writing tool tends to **assume** about a place (often a generic movie cemetery) when you have not filed real photos. |
| **Author coordinates** | Your private filing labels — chapter, line span, site name — so you can find this research months later. |

---

This folder holds **two real research images** from the maintainer's long-form novel project. Names and manuscript prose are omitted here on purpose; only the **map** and **Street View** pair remain, so you can see what "filed grounding" actually looks like.

These files go with the walkthrough in [`grounding-pov-blind-case-study.md`](../grounding-pov-blind-case-study.md). Read that first for the three-layer workflow; come back here when you want to open the images themselves.

**The scene (in plain terms):** A boy collapses beside a small prairie cemetery in North Dakota. He is barely conscious. The draft **never names** the cemetery — and that is **correct** for his point of view. The author's job is still to know the real roadside: open grass, no stone walls, no shade trees, a mowed patch easy to miss from the highway.

## What's in this folder

| File | What it is | Why you keep it |
|------|------------|-----------------|
| [`flaxton-bowbells-coteau.jpg`](flaxton-bowbells-coteau.jpg) | **Map** (Google Maps screenshot) | Shows the **corridor**: Flaxton toward Bowbells and Coteau, where the Danish Cemetery sits west of Bowbells, and how the highways connect. |
| [`danish-cemetery-dylan-collapse.jpg`](danish-cemetery-dylan-collapse.jpg) | **Street View** (2024 capture) | Shows **what the shoulder looks like**: patchy grassland, bald prairie, **no walls, no trees** — not a Gothic movie set. |

**Why both?** A map alone tells you *where*. Street View tells you *what it looks like when you stand there*. Together they stop the tool from inventing a stock cemetery (iron gates, spooky trees, mood lighting) while your character only feels gravel, heat, and pain.

When you revise a beat like this, open **both** images before you ask the tool to help with wording — or before you judge whether a paragraph "feels right."

## What each image is good for (and what it is not)

Think of this as an honest index: what you **may** rely on when checking prose, and what still requires your judgment or other notes.

### Map — `flaxton-bowbells-coteau.jpg`

| | |
|--|--|
| **Shows** | The drive corridor: US-52, the Bowbells junction, ND-8 south toward Coteau, and the **Danish Cemetery** landmark west of Bowbells. |
| **Modern labels** | Google Maps uses **today's** road names and town labels — fine for geometry; your story may use period-appropriate wording on the page. |
| **Does not settle** | Exact 1972 gravel highway naming on every segment; precise GPS for a fictional pickup spot. |
| **Story context** | In the maintainer's arc, this is chapter 3 "route overreach": the character rejects the easier paved highway and stays on the prairie road where the collapse happens — **beside** the cemetery, not a detour into Bowbells town. |

### Street View — `danish-cemetery-dylan-collapse.jpg`

| | |
|--|--|
| **Shows** | Open shoulder, patchy grass, grain fields, a **mowed patch** that reads as a small cemetery from the road — **no walls, no trees**. |
| **Capture date** | 2024 — use for **terrain and layout**, not for assuming every fence post or sign matched a historical year. |
| **Does not settle** | Gothic atmosphere, iron gates, tree-lined lanes, or any detail the photo frame does not show. |
| **Story context** | Collapse and red Ford pickup on the Hwy 15 shoulder. The site may **never** appear by name in the manuscript — grounding still applies to **you** when you revise. |

## When the tool pulls the wrong picture

Without filed photos, models often default to a **generic cemetery**: walls, iron gates, shade trees, mood weather. The real site in these images is the opposite — bald prairie, easy to drive past without naming.

| What the tool tends to invent | What these files document |
|------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Walls, iron gate | **No walls** |
| Spooky trees, shade | **No trees** |
| Cemetery as a set piece | **Mowed patch in grain fields** — easy to miss from the road |

**The question to ask while revising:** *"Does this roadside beat match what we documented — no walls, no trees?"*

**Not:** *"Did I type the word cemetery?"* A search of your chapter that returns **zero** hits for "cemetery" does **not** mean your grounding work was wasted.

## How you might file this in your own project

You do not need special software to start. A folder on your machine and one sentence in chat (or in your notes) is enough.

**One-line capture (you can adapt this):**

> *"Add this map and these Street View images to grounding for chapter 3 — Danish Cemetery near Coteau."*

**Chapter header (author coordinates — for your eyes, not necessarily the reader):**

`ch03 L62–end · collapse pickup · Danish Cemetery (POV-blind)`

That header is what lets you **find** this research again months later, when chat history is gone but the draft has moved on.
